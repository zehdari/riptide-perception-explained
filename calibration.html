Cameras and calibration

Before a pixel can say where something is, the software has to know exactly how the camera turned the world into that pixel. Those numbers are the calibration, and underwater the ones that come from the factory are wrong.

The short version
  • A camera is described by its intrinsics (focal length and image centre, in pixels) and its lens distortion. A stereo camera also has extrinsics: where its two lenses sit relative to each other.
  • Talos's ZED X Mini cameras look through a flat window into water. The window bends light, so the focal length, the distortion and even the stereo geometry are different from the factory's in-air calibration.
  • The team calibrates each camera underwater: photograph a ChArUco board from many angles with both lenses, let OpenCV solve for the numbers, and give the result to the ZED SDK.
  • Depth comes from how far a point shifts between the left and right images. A tiny matching error is a big depth error far away: it grows with the square of the distance.
  • Where each camera sits on the robot (camera → base_link) comes from CAD and a measured tilt, in the robot description, not from this calibration.

From a point to a pixel

Everything in the vision code assumes the simplest possible camera, a pinhole: every ray of light passes through one point, the optical centre, and lands on a flat sensor behind it. Put the camera frame at that centre, with z straight out of the lens, x to the right and y down (ROS calls this the optical frame, talos/ffc_left_camera_optical_frame). A point at (x, y, z) lands on pixel

u = f_x\,\frac{x}{z} + c_x, \qquad v = f_y\,\frac{y}{z} + c_y \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \propto \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{K} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}

The four numbers are the intrinsics. f_x and f_y are the focal length measured in pixels: how many pixels one unit of x/z covers. (c_x, c_y) is the principal point, the pixel the lens axis goes through, near but never exactly at the image centre. K is the same four numbers written as a matrix, which is how every library stores them. Talos's forward camera, calibrated underwater, has f_x = 1701.5, f_y = 1702.4, c_x = 935.2, c_y = 579.4 for its 1920×1200 image, so the picture is 2\arctan(960/1701.5) = 59° wide.

Going the other way is what the detector does: a pixel only tells you a direction, ((u - c_x)/f_x,\ (v - c_y)/f_y,\ 1). To get a point you need its distance too, which is what the depth image is for (Chapter 4).

Lenses bend straight lines

Real lenses are not pinholes. Rays far from the axis bend a little more or less than the pinhole model says, so straight edges near the corners of the image come out curved. OpenCV's standard model (the one the team's calibration files use, called plumb bob or Brown–Conrady) describes it with five numbers (k_1, k_2, p_1, p_2, k_3). Take the ideal pinhole coordinates x' = x/z, y' = y/z and their distance from the axis r^2 = x'^2 + y'^2; the lens moves them to

x'' = x'\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + 2p_1 x'y' + p_2(r^2 + 2x'^2), \qquad y'' = y'\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + p_1(r^2 + 2y'^2) + 2p_2 x'y'

and the pixel is then u = f_x x'' + c_x, v = f_y y'' + c_y. The k terms are radial: they push points straight out from the centre (positive: pincushion, edges bow inward) or pull them in (negative: barrel). The p terms are tangential, from a lens not quite parallel to the sensor. The ZED SDK uses these coefficients to rectify every image before publishing it, so the image_rect_color the detector sees is already a pinhole image, and the code can use the simple formula above.

Why water breaks the factory calibration

Every ZED leaves the factory calibrated, in air. Talos's cameras look out through a flat window, and light bends where it crosses from water into the air inside the housing. Snell's law says a ray at angle \theta_w from the axis in water travels at \theta_a inside, with

n_\text{air} \sin\theta_a = n_\text{water} \sin\theta_w, \qquad n_\text{water} \approx 1.33

so rays from the edge of the scene are bent outward, toward the edge of the sensor. Two things follow:

  • The field of view shrinks by about the factor 1.33, and the focal length in pixels grows by the same factor. That's why Talos's underwater f_x \approx 1700 is so much longer than the lens's in-air value.
  • Distortion appears. Snell's law is in sines, not angles, so the bending grows faster than linearly toward the edges: a strong pincushion that the in-air distortion coefficients know nothing about. It shows up as the large positive k_1 (about +0.27) in both of Talos's calibrations.

The stereo geometry changes too, because each lens's rays now bend at the window differently. A factory calibration underwater gives depth that is wrong by roughly the refraction factor and surfaces that come out curved or tilted. So the team calibrates underwater and turns the ZED SDK's self-calibration off (self_calib: false in each camera's config): left on, the SDK would "correct" the stereo geometry at start-up using its in-air assumptions.

Two lenses make depth

A ZED X Mini is two cameras 50 mm apart, looking the same way. A point at depth z appears in both images, but shifted sideways: the disparity d = u_\text{left} - u_\text{right}. Similar triangles give the depth from it:

z = \frac{f\,B}{d}, \qquad \delta z \approx \frac{z^2}{f\,B}\,\delta d

where B is the baseline, the distance between the lenses, and f the focal length in pixels. With Talos's f = 1701.5 px and B = 49.75 mm, a point 2 m away shifts 42 px between the images; at 6 m it shifts only 14. The second formula is why depth gets bad quickly with range: a matching error \delta d of a quarter pixel is 1.2 cm of depth at 2 m and 11 cm at 6 m. That z^2 is the reason mapping's noise model (Chapter 6) trusts depth much less than direction.

Finding which pixel in the right image matches each pixel in the left is the SDK's job, done on the GPU. It is only a 1D search if the two images are rectified: warped so that a point appears on the same row in both. To do that the SDK needs the stereo extrinsics: the rotation R and translation T from the left lens to the right. Talos's forward camera has T = (-49.75,\ 0.02,\ 0.98) mm and an R that turns the right lens about 1.35° about the vertical axis. Those are exactly what the stereo calibration measures.

Why a ChArUco board

Calibration needs pictures of something whose geometry is known exactly, seen from many angles. The solver finds the intrinsics, distortion and stereo extrinsics that best explain where every known point landed in every image. Two kinds of target are common, and each has a weakness:

  • A chessboard's inner corners can be located to a fraction of a pixel, because each is where four squares meet. But the detector has to see the whole board to know which corner is which, and a board that is partly out of frame or partly in murk is useless.
  • ArUco markers are little black-and-white codes, each with an ID, so any one that is visible identifies itself. But a marker's corners are less precise than a chessboard's.

A ChArUco board is a chessboard with an ArUco marker printed in each white square. The markers say which square is which; the chessboard corners between them are then located to sub-pixel accuracy, each with a known ID. So any visible part of the board gives precise, identified corners. Underwater that matters: the board is often at an angle, partly out of frame, or half lost in scattered light, and corners near the edges of the image, where distortion is strongest, are exactly the ones a chessboard calibration would struggle to get.

Calibrating, step by step

  1. Get the board in the water. It has to be rigid and flat (any bend is an error the solver can't see) and well lit.
  2. Capture stereo pairs. riptide_hardware2/picture_taker.py, launched by zed.launch.py, subscribes to the camera's stereo_raw/image_raw_color: the raw side-by-side image from both lenses, before any rectification, because calibration has to see what the lenses really do. Each call of its capture_image service saves the newest frame cut in half, as lossless PNGs with the same name in cal_images/left/ and cal_images/right/.
  3. Cover everything. Dozens of pairs, with the board near and far, tilted every way, and moved around so it visits the whole image, especially the corners. A solver only learns the distortion where it has seen corners.
  4. Solve. Detect the ChArUco corners in each image (OpenCV's aruco module), calibrate each lens on its own (calibrateCamera: K and distortion), then the pair (stereoCalibrate: R and T). Which coefficients to free is a choice: the current forward calibration fixes the tangential terms at zero and frees k_3; the 2026 downward one does the opposite.
  5. Check it. The RMS reprojection error (how far, on average, each detected corner is from where the solved model puts it) should be well under a pixel. The baseline should come out near 50 mm and R near the identity. If one image has a much larger error than the rest, drop it and solve again.
  6. Install it. Save the result as an OpenCV FileStorage YAML with Size, K_LEFT, D_LEFT, K_RIGHT, D_RIGHT, R and T (in mm) in riptide_hardware/cfg/, point the camera's optional_opencv_calibration_file at it in ffc_config.yaml or dfc_config.yaml, keep self_calib: false, and restart the ZED node. The SDK now rectifies with your numbers and publishes them on rgb/camera_info, which is where the detector reads its K.

The solver script itself and the board's exact dimensions live on the robot (/home/ros/zed_cals), not in the repositories. The files it produced are in riptide_hardware/cfg/: the forward camera uses tirpak_ffc_charuco_3.yaml, the downward camera zedxm_dfc_2026_cal.yaml.

Where the cameras are on the robot

The calibration says how each camera maps the world to pixels, measured from that camera. To put a detection on the course map, mapping also needs where each camera is on Talos: the transform from base_link to the camera's optical frame. That one is not calibrated. It comes from the robot description, riptide_descriptions/config/talos.yaml, with positions from CAD and tilts measured on the robot, and the URDF publishes it as a fixed TF transform.

  • Forward camera: about 11 cm ahead of base_link, 12 cm to starboard and 10 cm up, pitched 0.05 rad (2.9°) down.
  • Downward camera: level with base_link fore and aft, 12 cm to starboard, pointing straight down.

These matter as much as the calibration. A 1° error in the forward camera's pitch puts a prop 3.5 cm too high or low at 2 m, and every detection inherits it. The optical frame (z forward, y down) is turned relative to base_link (x forward, z up); the URDF handles that turn, and code should always go through TF rather than swapping axes by hand.

How a bad calibration shows up

  • Flat things come out tilted or curved. The detector fits a plane to each prop (Chapter 4); a wrong distortion or stereo geometry bends the depth surface, and the normals point off.
  • Distances are off by a constant factor. A wrong focal length or baseline scales depth. Props seem nearer or farther than they are, consistently.
  • Straight edges are curved in image_rect_color. Rectification is supposed to make them straight.
  • Depth has holes on textured surfaces. Bad rectification means the matcher searches the wrong row.

The detector's README gives the same first suggestion for orientations that come out slightly off: check that the camera is using the right calibration file.
