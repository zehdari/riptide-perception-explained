Chapter 2

From a mask to a pose

A mask says which pixels are the object. The depth image says how far away each pixel is. Put together, they give a few hundred 3D points on the object's surface, and a plane through them gives its position and the way it faces.

Almost every RoboSub prop Talos looks for is flat, or has a flat face that matters: a printed symbol on a board, the side of a basket, the top of a cylinder on the table. The detector's generic path assumes exactly that. For each box it samples the masked pixels, turns them into 3D points with the depth image, throws away the points that don't belong, and fits a plane (detection.py, "Shared surface fitting"; the filtering is in pointcloud.py). Sampling the mask

Before any box is processed, every mask in the frame is painted into one full-frame mask image. For each box the detector takes the part of that image inside the box (shrunk by class_detect_shrink on each side, which Talos sets to 0) and keeps a regular grid of its pixels: every grid_step = 8 px across and down, the ones that are inside a mask. A box 200 px on a side has 625 grid pixels; the masked ones are typically half to three quarters of those. If more than max_sample_points = 500 survive, 500 are kept, evenly spaced through the list (np.linspace over the indices), so a box close to the camera costs the same as one at mid-range.

Because the mask image is the union of every mask in the frame, a box samples any masked pixel inside it, its own object's or a neighbour's. In the Nereus frames here no box overlaps another object's mask, so it never happens; two objects overlapping in the image, at different depths, would mix their samples, and the depth gate below is what would separate them.

Back-projection

Each sampled pixel (u, v) with a valid depth z (not zero, NaN or infinite, which is what the ZED reports where stereo matching failed) becomes a point in the camera's optical frame by inverting the pinhole projection:

x = \frac{(u - c_x)\, z}{f_x}, \qquad y = \frac{(v - c_y)\, z}{f_y}, \qquad \bp = (x,\, y,\, z)

f_x, f_y: focal lengths in pixels, (c_x, c_y): the principal point, both from camera_info. Nereus's forward camera: 1864 px and (956, 585).

The depth is the z coordinate, so no normalisation by ray length is needed. One pixel at 2 m spans 2 / 1864 ≈ 1.1 mm, so the 8 px grid puts samples about 9 mm apart on an object two metres away.

The depth gate

A mask is never exactly the object. Its outline is a few pixels loose, and a stereo camera blurs depth across the edge of an object: the pixels just outside a board read somewhere between the board and the wall behind it (stereo practitioners call them flying pixels). A sample on such a pixel lands metres behind the object, and a plane fitted with it tilts toward the background. The first filter removes them by depth alone (_depth_filter):

m = \operatorname{median}(z_i), \qquad s = \max\!\big(1.4826\,\operatorname{median}|z_i - m|,\; 0.05\ \text{m}\big), \qquad \text{keep } |z_i - m| \le 3\,s

The median and the median absolute deviation (MAD) are robust: a third of the points could sit on the wall and neither would move much. 1.4826 scales the MAD to the standard deviation it equals for Gaussian data, so depth_mad_scale = 3 is a three-sigma gate. The 5 cm floor (depth_min_spread) matters for a board seen head-on: all its points are at almost the same depth, the MAD is millimetres, and without the floor a gate that tight would cut real points on a slightly tilted board. With the floor, nothing within 15 cm of the median depth is ever removed.

The gate looks only at z, so it adapts to distance automatically and costs one sort. It is two-sided: a stray point in front of the object goes too.

Two outlier passes

The survivors then go through two neighbourhood filters (_outlier_indices, with the masks from geometry.py):

  • Radius pass. Keep a point if more than k = \min(10,\, 0.8\,n) points (itself included) lie within 1 m of it.
  • Statistical pass, on the radius survivors. For each point, the mean distance to its k nearest neighbours; drop the points whose mean distance is more than one standard deviation above the average.

Both are standard point-cloud filters, and both behave in ways worth knowing on a patch this small. A 1 m radius is larger than almost every prop, so the radius pass only ever removes a point more than a metre from nearly all the others, which the depth gate has usually removed already. The statistical pass does the opposite: its threshold is relative, so on a perfectly clean patch it still removes the points with the sparsest neighbourhoods, the ones on the rim of the grid. In the Nereus frames on this page it drops a median of 13 % of a patch (up to 21 %), almost all of it along the outline. That costs a little information at the edges but never tilts the plane. One far-off point that survives the gate, though, inflates the standard deviation so much that the statistical pass removes only that point and keeps the rim: the first pass makes the second one predictable.

The plane fit

The kept points (at least min_points = 5) define the surface. fit_plane is the least-squares plane by singular value decomposition: subtract the centroid, decompose, and the direction of least variance is the normal.

\bar\bp = \frac1n \sum_i \bp_i, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} (\bp_1 - \bar\bp)^\top \\ \vdots \\ (\bp_n - \bar\bp)^\top \end{bmatrix} = U \Sigma V^\top, \qquad \bn = V_{:,3}

The last right-singular vector minimises the sum of squared distances from the points to the plane through \bar\bp, so this is orthogonal regression, which treats depth error and image-plane error alike. The sign of an SVD vector is arbitrary; the detector flips it to face the camera (n_z < 0, since the camera looks along +z).

The orientation published with the detection is built so that its +z axis is the normal (quat_from_normal_stable). Its x and y axes come from a fixed rule (cross the normal with the camera's x axis, or with y when the normal is nearly along x), so the same normal always gives the same quaternion and there is no singularity anywhere. The roll about the normal carries no information: a plane has no "up". Mapping uses only the direction of the normal, as the object's heading (Chapter 5).

Where the object is

The position is not the mean of the points. It is the centre of the bounding box, back-projected at the mean depth of the points:

\mathbf c = \Big(\tfrac{(u_b - c_x)\, \bar z}{f_x},\; \tfrac{(v_b - c_y)\, \bar z}{f_y},\; \bar z\Big), \qquad (u_b, v_b) = \text{box centre}, \qquad \bar z = \bar\bp_z

The box centre is a stable image position for the object even when filtering removes points unevenly. Note that this is not where the ray through the box centre meets the fitted plane (ray_plane_intersection, which the torpedo board uses). The two agree when the box centre's ray hits the plane at the patch's mean depth, which is nearly always true: the box centre and the patch's centre are close in the image. On the 37° board in the figure the difference is under half a centimetre. It grows with the tilt times the gap between the two centres, which gets large when the mask is lopsided, for example an object cut off by the edge of the image. Then the box centre is the centre of the visible part, which is a bigger error anyway, and one the mapping's acquisition rules exist to survive.

What comes out

Each box that survives with five or more points becomes one Detection3D: the class, the box's confidence, the centroid and the quaternion, all in the camera's optical frame. The detection carries no covariance; how much one of them is worth is decided downstream (Chapter 4). A box whose points don't survive publishes nothing, and that is the common failure mode: an object whose mask lies mostly on invalid depth (the edge of the image, a reflective surface, open water with nothing behind it) is seen by the network but never reaches mapping.

The same samples, after filtering, also feed the debug point cloud and the dots drawn on the annotated image, so what you see in RViz or Foxglove is exactly what the plane was fitted to.
