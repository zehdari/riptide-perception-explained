Mapping · Chapter 10

One mission in Nereus

Everything in the earlier chapters, in one run: Talos's whole software stack swimming the RoboSub 2026 course in the simulator, on a course that is not where its map says.

The short version
  • Talos's full software stack ran the competition tree in Nereus twice: once on the course its map describes, once with the course moved 72 cm and the torpedo board another 1.27 m.
  • Seeing the slalom put the whole map in the right place, exactly as intended.
  • The torpedo was then explained partly as odometry drift instead of being written off, and that pulled every prop not yet seen back off the truth.
  • That in turn hid one of the table's baskets: the down camera was pointed at the wrong spot, the basket only appeared 2–3 frames at a time, and mapping needs 4 that agree. No basket, no table fit.
  • Both runs still finished the tree. Every task fails soft and moves on, which is why the failures are only visible in the data.

The run

Nereus simulates the pool, the props, Talos's dynamics and sensors, and renders both ZED cameras' colour and depth images. Everything else is the robot's own software, unchanged: the YOLO models and tensor_detector, mapping, the EKF, the MPC, and the competition behaviour tree RepairCompMpcTree, started the way the team starts it at the pool. Nothing in the stack knows where the simulator put the props.

Talos's map is riptide_mapping's init_data: the RoboSub 2026 layout as the team measured it. The run was flown twice.

  • Exact course. Nereus puts every prop exactly where the map says.
  • Moved course. Every prop is shifted 0.6 m east and 0.4 m south (72 cm), as if the whole layout had been measured from the wrong corner, and the torpedo board another 0.9 m east and 0.9 m north on top of that: a prop measured badly. The shared shift is the offset mapping estimates; the torpedo's extra 1.27 m is a layout deviation.

Both runs finished the tree with status SUCCESS. That sounds better than it is: every task in the tree fails soft (Chapter 9), so a search that times out just hands over to the next task. On the moved course two things went wrong that only the data shows, and they are the most useful part of this chapter.

One thing the replay shows that is not a problem: at the gate the forward camera reports a slalom pole on one of the gate's legs, a false detection (Chapter 3). The tree keeps the map locked through the gate, so mapping ignores it, and even unlocked it would only be fused if slalom_parent were the target. That is what targets are for.

What went wrong 1: the torpedo moves the whole map

In short: the slalom put the map in the right place, then the badly placed torpedo was explained mostly as the robot having drifted, which dragged every prop Talos had not looked at yet back off the truth.

The first prop mapping is asked to find is the slalom; the gate is driven through straight from its prior and never targeted. 65 s into the run the slalom's first four detections agree, and after ten it is confirmed and its track becomes the map's first fix. One sighting can't tell a moved course from one badly placed prop, so the solve splits the 72 cm by the two uncertainties, and almost all of it goes to the offset (94 % by the configured priors; in simulation the offset's start prior is effectively unlimited, see the end of this chapter): (0.57, −0.38) m against the (0.60, −0.40) m Nereus applied. Every prop the map has not seen moves with it, so the torpedo, the bins and the table are all predicted 68 cm closer to the truth before Talos has looked at them.

Then the torpedo. Its prior is 1.58 m from where it really is; after the offset, 1.27 m. That is not far enough from the map's prediction to need the long, 12-detection acquisition, because the prediction is wide by then: 23 m of travel since the slalom (about 21 cm of drift per axis at drift_var_per_m_xy = 0.002) plus the 2° course-yaw prior, which is 63 cm at the torpedo's 18 m from the origin. Four detections agree, the torpedo is confirmed and becomes a second fix, and the solve now has two props that disagree.

It does not release the torpedo. Its model has cheaper explanations than a 5σ layout error: odometry that drifted 20-odd cm between the two sightings, and a small turn of the whole layout (the course yaw). The newest offset node, the one every unseen prop hangs from, jumps to (1.13, −0.12) m: 56 cm east and 25 cm north, part of the way toward the torpedo's extra (0.9, 0.9) m. The torpedo itself ends 11 cm from the truth, but the gate, the bins and the table, predicted from that offset, end 62–77 cm off: no better than their priors. In Nereus the odometry really drifted less than 15 cm over the whole run, so here the drift model was the wrong explanation. On the robot, with real drift, it may well be the right one. Which one the solve picks depends on drift_var_per_m_xy, course_yaw_std_deg and layout_std_xy, and a robot run with measured drift is the way to set them.

What went wrong 2: a basket that was never found

In short: because the map was off, the down camera kept looking at the wrong spot for the warning basket. It only ever caught the basket for two or three frames at a time, and mapping won't start tracking a new target until four detections agree within four seconds. So the basket never got an estimate, and the table was never fit to its baskets.

The table task (Chapter 9) searches for the two baskets with DownwardSearchMpc: hover the down camera 1.5 m over the basket's current estimate, follow it while it is in view, otherwise spiral out around the estimate, for up to 60 s each. The helmet basket went fine: found at 3:39, its error at 0.00023, under the 0.0005 it needed (about 1.2 cm σ). Then the warning basket.

Its estimate came from the map: the prior position on the table, carried by the table's frame, which hangs off the offset the torpedo had just pulled 60 cm the wrong way. Mapping had the warning basket 69 cm from where it really was. The tree flew the camera over that spot and spiralled. Every 14 s the spiral swept the edge of the camera's view across the real basket: the tree logged warning in view: following the detection, the detector reported it two or three times, and then it was gone again, because "following" means re-aiming at warning_frame, which had not moved.

And it couldn't move. A target that has just been reset holds its first detections until 4 agree within 0.3 m over the last 4 s (acquisition by agreement, Chapter 7). Two or three every 14 s never makes four, so every burst aged out unused, the estimate stayed at the prior, the published covariance stayed at the unconfirmed 1.73, and after 60 s the search gave up. The tree's one-second check that both baskets are converged failed, it logged helmet,warning, not both converged below 0.0005: the table keeps its prior, and center_table_on_baskets never ran. On the exact course the same task found both baskets and fit the table: Moved table by (-0.058, 0.035, -0.001) in map to fit the baskets, yawed table 1.0 deg.

Nothing here is a bug on its own. Each rule is sensible: hold back the first detections of a new target, search around the estimate, check the baskets before trusting them. Together they deadlock when the estimate starts more than about half a camera footprint off. Ways out, any one of which would have worked here: have the search steer by the raw detection, not the mapping frame, until the target is acquired; keep held detections longer than 4 s (or count agreeing bursts across glances); or, upstream, keep the torpedo from moving the offset (Chapter 8).

What the simulator does and doesn't show

  • Depth is clean. Nereus renders depth from its geometry with mild noise, so detections scatter about ten times less than the noise model assumes (Chapter 6), and the depth gate rarely has anything to remove (Chapter 4). The robot's stereo depth is noisier and fails on texture-less water and walls.
  • The odometry is good. The simulated DVL and gyro are close to ideal, so the EKF drifted less than 15 cm over the 133 m run. Any drift the solve assumes is its own explanation, not something that happened. On the robot, the offset also has to absorb real drift.
  • The start-time drift is large. In simulation the node reads the clock before the first /clock message arrives, so its first predict adds drift for the whole epoch, about 1.8 × 109 s, to the offset's prior (the 17 900 m² it publishes at the start). That makes the offset's start prior unlimited in Nereus. On the robot, with wall time, it starts at the configured 1 m. With the configured prior a first sighting would put 94 % of a shift on the offset rather than nearly all of it, so the runs here are barely affected, but it is worth fixing in mapping.py (take last_predict_time on the first odometry or detection, not in the constructor).
  • Everything else is real. The models, thresholds, filters, the tree's covariance checks and its timing are what run on Talos.

The data for this chapter and the overview's replay come from ros2 bag recordings of the runs (tools/bag_to_mission.py), with mapping's log lines lined up to mission time.
