Chapter 3

Props made of several pieces

The network sees symbols, not props. A torpedo board is four symbols and four identical rings; a gate half is two signs. Some objects mapping needs can only be built once the whole frame has been seen.

Chapter 2's generic path makes one detection per box. Three groups of classes are held back from it and resolved together after every box in the frame has been routed (process): pairs of symbols that form one prop, the torpedo board, and the slalom poles. Each produces detections under names the network never outputs, gate_repair, torpedo, fire_hole_large, and those are the names mapping tracks.

Pairs

The 2026 gate has two halves, each marked by a sign with two symbols on it: compass and hammer-and-wrench for the "repair" side, buoy and SOS for "rescue". Autonomy needs the half, not the symbols, so the detector merges them (DETECTION_PAIRS):

  • compass + hammer_and_wrench → gate_repair
  • buoy + sos → gate_rescue
  • warning + helmet → table, only after set_table_pair_enabled(true)

When both members are in the frame (_resolve_pairs, _emit_combined_pair), each member box is sampled on its own, exactly as in Chapter 2, the samples are pooled, and one plane is fitted to all of them. The position is the centre of the union of the boxes, back-projected at the pooled points' mean depth, which lands between the two symbols on the sign; the confidence is the lower of the members'. Pooling helps the normal twice: there are more points, and they span a wider stretch of the sign, so a small error in depth tilts the plane less.

When only one member is visible, the pair is not formed: that box goes down the generic path under its own name (sos, compass) like any other class. Mapping has entries for those names too, so a lone symbol still says where the sign is, only under a different name. In the Nereus mission's gate frames the network found sos in one frame and compass in the next, each without its partner, at confidences just above the 0.65 threshold: the far signs are small, and a pair needs both members to clear the threshold in the same frame.

The table pair

The table is the one pair whose orientation is not the plane's. The two baskets, warning and helmet, stand at opposite ends of the table (0.8 m apart in the mapping config), and what autonomy wants from a table detection is a frame lying flat on it, so that "drop over the table" means the same thing from any viewpoint. _table_pair_quat builds it in the world frame:

  1. Intersect the rays through each basket's box centre with the fitted plane, giving 3D points \mathbf w (warning) and \mathbf h (helmet).
  2. Rotate \mathbf h - \mathbf w into map using the camera's TF, and drop its vertical part.
  3. Table +z is world up; table +x is world up crossed with that horizontal warning→helmet direction, so +x points across the table, at an edge with no basket.
  4. Rotate back into the camera frame for publishing.

The torpedo board

The torpedo board carries four symbols, fire, blood, an ambulance and a fire engine, and four openings: for each of fire and blood, one large and one small, near that symbol. A torpedo through the right opening scores. The network finds the openings only as circle, four boxes with the same class and the same look. Which one is "fire, large" is decided by geometry, in process_torpedo_task. It runs only on the forward camera (torpedo_task_camera), so on the downward camera fire and blood are ordinary classes, the bin vinyls.

One plane for the whole board. Fire and blood are required: if neither fits, nothing is published. Each symbol that is present, fire, blood and whichever of ambulance and fire engine were seen, is sampled and filtered as in Chapter 2, and their points are pooled into one SVD plane. A single symbol at the edge of the image can give a badly tilted plane on its own (in one mission frame a fire symbol cut off by the image edge fits almost 50° off the board's normal); pooled with the others it barely matters. The plane passes through the mean of the fire and blood centroids.

Every centre on the plane. The centre pixel of each symbol box and each circle box is turned into a 3D point by intersecting its camera ray with that plane:

\mathbf d = \frac{K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^\top}{\lVert K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^\top \rVert}, \qquad t = \frac{\bn \cdot \mathbf a}{\bn \cdot \mathbf d}, \qquad \mathbf p = t\,\mathbf d

\mathbf a: the anchor point on the plane, \bn: the board normal. The circles have no usable surface of their own (an opening's middle is open water), so this is the only way to place them.

The board centre. With an in-plane basis (\hat u, \hat v), every projected centre gets coordinates (u_k, v_k); the torpedo detection is the middle of their extent, \big(\tfrac{\min u_k + \max u_k}{2}, \tfrac{\min v_k + \max v_k}{2}\big). With every symbol and opening in view, that is the middle of the printed pattern. With fewer, it is the middle of what was seen: in the mission's first frame of the board, from about 2 m, only fire cleared the threshold, and the torpedo detection went out at the fire symbol, 30 cm from the board's centre. Mapping's tracking (Chapter 5) treats that as one more noisy detection; it is a bias, not noise, while only part of the board is visible. Its confidence is the lower of fire's and blood's, and its orientation is the board normal's.

Naming the openings. Only with fire, blood and exactly four circles in the frame. Project each opening onto the axis from the fire symbol to the blood symbol, s_k = (\mathbf p_k - \mathbf p_{\text{fire}}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf s}, and sort: the two smallest s are fire's openings, the two largest blood's. Within each pair, the one nearer its symbol in 3D is the large one. Sorting along the symbol axis always gives a 2–2 split, and it works with distances measured on the board in 3D, so it doesn't care how the board is rolled or where the camera is. Rules in image coordinates are fragile. Even "each opening belongs to the symbol nearest it in the image", which is right head-on and at any roll, fails once the board is turned far enough for perspective to squeeze its far side.

Each opening is published with the board's orientation and a marker sized from its box (pixels / 150, a rough metres-at-typical-range scale for RViz only). The slalom

A slalom pole is a thin vertical pipe: there is no surface to fit a plane to, and the mask is a few pixels wide. The slalom path (create_detection3d_message, _process_slalom) is correspondingly simple:

  • The position of each pole is one depth sample, at the box's centre pixel, back-projected. A NaN or zero depth there (the centre pixel missing the pole) drops the pole for this frame.
  • Of the poles in a frame, only the closest (smallest depth) is kept. slalom_history_size is 1 on Talos, so the "closest in history" is simply the closest this frame.
  • The orientation is not measured at all: it is the rotation of slalom_parent_frame, mapping's prior for the slalom row, looked up in TF and expressed in the camera frame, then turned −90° about y so the marker arrow lies along the row. Without that TF the node warns and publishes the placeholder orientation.
  • It goes out under the name set by set_slalom_type (default slalom_front). The mission trees set it to slalom_parent, so in practice the closest pole updates mapping's estimate of the whole slalom row, and the three poles hang off that at their config offsets.

Mapping locks every slalom frame's orientation to its config (lock_orientation_to_config), so the published orientation never reaches the estimate anyway; only the position counts.
