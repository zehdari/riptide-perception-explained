Training the detector

The neural network that finds the props doesn't know anything about RoboSub until the team shows it thousands of labelled pictures. How those pictures are made decides most of what the detector gets right and wrong.

The short version
  • Talos runs a YOLO segmentation model: for every object it finds, a class, a confidence, a box and a pixel mask. One model per camera.
  • Training data comes from two places: videos of the real props, with masks labelled by SAM (a model that segments an object you click on and follows it through the video), and synthetic images from Nereus, labelled automatically.
  • The labelled images are then augmented: rotated, zoomed, cropped, mirrored, and props cut out and pasted onto unrelated pictures so the model learns what is not a prop. Some classes skip some augmentations: the slalom poles are never rotated, because they are always vertical.
  • Ultralytics adds its own augmentations during training (mosaic, colour, scale). Training starts from a model already trained on everyday objects (fine-tuning).
  • The result is a .pt file; on the robot it is compiled into a TensorRT engine and named in yolo_orientation.yaml.

What the network learns

The detector (Chapter 3) runs instance segmentation: it doesn't just say "there is a torpedo board symbol somewhere", it outlines every pixel of each one. Talos needs the outline because the next step (Chapter 4) samples depth only inside it; a box would also include the pool wall behind the prop.

The models are Ultralytics YOLO26n-seg, the "nano" size: about as small as a segmentation model gets, so it runs in real time on the robot's Jetson Orin. Training doesn't start from scratch. It starts from yolo26n-seg.pt, already trained by Ultralytics on the COCO dataset's 80 everyday classes, and fine-tunes it: the network already knows edges, textures and shapes, and only has to learn which of them are RoboSub props. That is why a few thousand images are enough.

Each camera gets its own model, because each sees different things from a different angle: the forward model has eleven classes (gate signs, slalom pole, torpedo symbols and openings, bin magnets), the downward model eight (bin vinyls and the table's items and baskets). The class lists are in yolo_orientation.yaml, and a dataset's class order has to match them exactly.

Recording the props

Most of the training data starts as video. The team films each prop with the robot's own cameras, recording ZED .svo files, so the pictures have the same lens, the same colour response and the same water as the robot will see: from near and far, from the side and head-on, in good water and murky, under different lighting, with the robot moving. Video is efficient: one minute is a thousand frames from slightly different viewpoints. (The svo_processor script in the workspace converts SVO recordings into rectified video.)

Variety matters more than quantity. A thousand frames of the gate from the same spot teach the model one picture of the gate; a hundred from all around it teach it what a gate looks like.

Labelling masks with SAM

Drawing an outline around every prop in every frame by hand would take weeks. Instead the team uses SAM 3 (Meta's Segment Anything Model): click on an object, or describe it, in one frame, and SAM draws its mask and follows it through the rest of the video. A person then checks the masks, fixes the frames where SAM drifted or missed, and assigns each mask its class. The labelling rules (what counts as a class, what to do with partly hidden props) are in the team's computer vision labelling guide, written for CVAT.

The labels end up in the YOLO segmentation format: one text file per image, one line per object: the class number, then the outline as a list of (x, y) points, each divided by the image width or height so they lie between 0 and 1.

# labels/train/torpedo_004908.txt: class, then x y pairs (fractions of the image)
4 0.5099 0.1583 0.5073 0.1725 0.5047 0.1817 0.5036 0.1825 ...
0 0.6915 0.3958 0.6889 0.4016 ...

Synthetic images from Nereus

The Nereus simulator can also generate training data, and its labels are free: it knows which object every pixel belongs to. nereus-dataset generate takes a dataset spec, places the camera around each task by a sampler (approach: a range, bearing and elevation from the prop, with some roll and pitch; overhead: an altitude above it for the downward camera), randomizes the water and light (four environments: nominal, clear, murky, overcast, with tint, scattering, caustics, sun angle, noise and blur), renders the image and a per-pixel object map, and turns the map into YOLO polygons. Views where a prop is too far, too small or broken into pieces are rejected.

The 2026 forward-camera set has 10 250 images (gate 2300, slalom 1500, torpedo 2000, octagon 1200, bins 2000, and 1250 backgrounds: pool views with no prop in them); the downward set 7350. Backgrounds are what teach a model that an empty pool wall is not a prop. Synthetic images are cheap and perfectly labelled, but they are not the real water, so they complement recorded video rather than replace it.

Augmenting: more variety from the same images

However much is recorded, the model will meet views it never saw: the gate from a new angle, the torpedo board half out of frame, a strange light. Augmentation fakes those by changing the training images (and their labels with them). The team does most of it offline, before training, producing the *_dataset_Augmented sets:

  • Rotation: the robot rolls, so the same prop appears tilted.
  • Zoom and crop: the same prop nearer and farther, and partly out of frame.
  • Mirroring: left-right flips double the views for free. (Careful with anything whose left and right differ.)
  • Copy-paste onto unrelated images: cut a prop out along its mask and paste it onto a picture from a general dataset like COCO, full of people, furniture, cars and animals. The model sees props in front of thousands of things that are not props, and learns to ignore them. This is the main defence against false detections, like the gate leg the forward model calls a slalom pole in Chapter 3.

Augmentations are applied per class where reality says so. The slalom poles are never rotated: in the pool they are always vertical, so a tilted red pole is something the model should never see, and showing it would teach it to call tilted red things slalom poles.

Training

Training itself is one Ultralytics command, pointed at a data.yaml that lists the image folders and the class names in order:

yolo segment train model=yolo26n-seg.pt data=data.yaml imgsz=640 epochs=80 close_mosaic=10

Each epoch shows the network every training image once and nudges its weights to make its masks and classes closer to the labels. Ultralytics adds its own random augmentation to every image every time it is shown, so no epoch sees exactly the same pictures. The deployed models used Ultralytics' defaults:

SettingValueWhat it does
imgsz640images are resized (letterboxed) to 640 × 640, the size the robot runs at
epochs, close_mosaic80, 1080 passes; the last 10 without mosaic, so the model finishes on whole images
mosaic1.0every image is part of a 2 × 2 collage of four random training images
scale, translate0.5, 0.1random zoom ±50 % and shift ±10 %
fliplr, flipud0.5, 0half the images mirrored left-right; never upside down
hsv_h, hsv_s, hsv_v0.015, 0.7, 0.4random hue, saturation and brightness: different water and light
degrees, copy_paste0, 0off: rotation and copy-paste are done offline, where they can be per class

The team tracks runs in Weights & Biases. The forward model on the robot, rs26_ffc_finals, was trained this way for 80 epochs.

Reading the results

Before training, the labelled images are split: about 80 % to train on and 20 % held back for validation, which the network never learns from. Every score is measured on the validation images, because a model can always do well on pictures it has memorised. Split by recording, not by frame: two neighbouring frames of one video are nearly the same picture, and having one in each set makes the score look better than it is.

  • Precision: of the things the model detected, how many were real. Low precision is false detections.
  • Recall: of the real things, how many the model found. Low recall is missed props.
  • mAP50: sweep the confidence threshold from high to low and precision falls as recall rises; the area under that curve is the class's average precision, and mAP50 is its mean over the classes, counting a detection as right if its mask overlaps the label by at least 50 %. mAP50-95 averages that over overlap requirements from 50 % to 95 %, so it also rewards precise outlines.

rs26_ffc_finals scored mask precision 0.952, recall 0.968, mAP50 0.948 and mAP50-95 0.880 on its validation set. High numbers are necessary, not sufficient: a validation set of the same pools and lighting as the training set says little about a new pool. If the validation score keeps rising while the robot does worse in the water, the model is overfitting to the training pictures. More varied data, more backgrounds, and testing on a recording the model never saw are the cures.

Putting a model on Talos

  1. Copy the trained .pt into tensor_detector/weights/.
  2. In config/yolo_orientation.yaml, set ffc_model or dfc_model to its file name, and the camera's class_id_map to its classes, in the model's order. The detector names classes from this map, not from the model file, and drops any id it doesn't list.
  3. Leave export: true. The first time the node starts on the robot it compiles the model into a TensorRT engine (.engine, next to the .pt) for the Orin's GPU, and uses the engine from then on. If export fails, it falls back to the .pt (Chapter 3). An engine only works on the GPU and TensorRT version that built it, so rebuild it after an upgrade.
  4. Check the thresholds (ffc_threshold, ffc_iou) against the new model's confidences, then watch ~/annotated/compressed in the pool, or in Nereus first.
