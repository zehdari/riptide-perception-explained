Chapter 4

How far to trust a detection

The detector says where an object is. It doesn't say how sure it is. Mapping fills that in with a noise model: a covariance for every detection that depends on how far away the object was, which way the camera was looking, and how fast Talos was moving when the frame was taken.

A Detection3D from tensor_detector carries a class, a confidence score and a pose in the camera's optical frame. Its covariance fields are left at zero. To fuse detections with a Kalman filter (Chapter 5), mapping needs to know how much each one should move the estimate, and that is what a measurement covariance \Rm encodes. A detection 80 cm away is worth far more than one 5 m away, and a frame grabbed mid-turn is worth less than one taken while holding still.

The model is one function, detection_covariance in map_estimator.py, called from update_object_with_pose for every detection mapping fuses. Its parameters live under noise: in config/config.yaml. Every figure on this page calls the browser's copy of that function.

The noise model

Split a stereo measurement into two directions: along the camera ray, which is the depth the stereo match measured, and across it, which is the bearing, where on the image the object sat. They fail differently, so the model gives each its own standard deviation. With r the detection's distance from the camera:

\sigma_\perp = b + k_\perp\, r, \qquad \sigma_\parallel = b + k_\parallel\, r + k_2\, r^2

The base term b = 1 cm is error any detection has, however close. Across the ray, a bearing error of a fixed angle becomes a sideways distance proportional to range, so k_\perp = 0.003 m per m: a bearing error of 3 milliradians (0.17°, about five pixels at Nereus's focal length), mostly from where the mask's edges fall. Along the ray, depth gets both a linear term, k_\parallel = 0.008 m per m, and a quadratic one, k_2 = 0.003 m per m².

Why depth error grows with the square of range

A stereo camera doesn't measure depth directly. It measures disparity: how many pixels a point shifts between the left and right images. With focal length f in pixels and baseline B between the two lenses, depth is z = fB/d. Differentiate it and a fixed matching error \delta d becomes

\delta z = \frac{z^2}{f B}\,\delta d

so depth error grows with the square of range. The ZED X Mini has a 50 mm baseline, and in Nereus the cameras render at 1920×1200 with f = 1864 px, so fB ≈ 93 px·m. One pixel of disparity error at 2 m is 4.3 cm of depth. The model's k_2 = 0.003 m per m² corresponds to matching to about a quarter of a pixel. The detector then averages up to 500 sampled points over the object's mask before it reports a depth (Chapter 2), which shrinks independent pixel noise. It doesn't shrink errors the points share, though, such as a depth map that is a little off everywhere on a surface.

Motion turns timing error into position error

A detection is stamped with its image's capture time, and mapping looks up the camera's pose at that stamp in TF to put the detection in the world. If the stamp and the pose disagree by \delta t, the detection lands where the camera would have put it \delta t earlier or later. While Talos turns at \omega, that swings it sideways by \omega\,\delta t\, r. While it moves at v, everything shifts by v\,\delta t. The model adds both with t_s = 0.03 s, the assumed stamp error:

\sigma_\perp^2 \mathrel{+}= (\omega\, t_s\, r)^2, \qquad \Rm_\text{cam} \mathrel{+}= (v\, t_s)^2\, I

The rates come from odometry. Mapping keeps the last 200 odometry messages as (stamp, |ω|, |v|) and uses the one nearest the detection's stamp (motion_at). Turning matters far more than moving: at 0.5 rad/s, an object 3 m away gets 4.5 cm of extra sideways error, more than twice its at-rest bearing error and most of its 6.1 cm depth error. Driving at 0.3 m/s adds 9 mm in every direction.

Putting it together

With \hat u = \bp_\text{cam}/r the unit ray to the detection, the covariance in the camera frame is the lateral variance in every direction, with the ray direction replaced by the along-ray variance. Mapping then rotates it into the frame the object is estimated in (map for a course prop, torpedo_frame for a torpedo hole), using the same TF rotation \Rm_{pc} that moved the detection there:

\Rm_\text{cam} = \sigma_\perp^2 I + (\sigma_\parallel^2 - \sigma_\perp^2)\,\hat u \hat u^\top + (v t_s)^2 I, \qquad \Rm = \Rm_{pc}\, \Rm_\text{cam}\, \Rm_{pc}^\top

The result is an ellipsoid, a cigar along the line of sight that fattens sideways while Talos turns. Drag the detection below to see it from above.

The same numbers as curves. At rest the along-ray error passes the across-ray error at once and keeps pulling away: by 4 m depth is four times worse than bearing. That shape is why a detection taken off to the side pins an object's position along a direction the one from straight ahead couldn't, and why the filter in Chapter 5 weighs each detection's ellipse rather than a single number.

Where the numbers came from

These defaults weren't fit to data. The config comment says so: "From the old thresholds (the holes' 0.0005 needed under ~2 cm of scatter at 1.5 m); measure the real scatter to tune." The behavior trees were written against the previous mapping, which published the spread of a buffer of recent detections as its covariance. The torpedo task, for example, waited for its holes' variance to fall under 0.0005 m², a standard deviation of 2.2 cm. The new noise model was set so the new filter passes those same checks after a similar amount of looking. At 1.5 m the model gives 2.9 cm along the ray and 1.5 cm across it.

The right way to set them is to measure. Hold Talos still in front of a prop, record detections, and compare their scatter with the curves at that range, then repeat at a few ranges and while turning. The figure below does that in Nereus.

The simulator's detections scatter about ten times less than the model allows: half a centimetre along the ray at 4 m where the model assumes 8.7. That is not evidence the model is too cautious on the robot. Nereus renders depth from its own geometry with mild noise, without the stereo matcher's failures on low-texture water and pool walls, and a 15 s hold only measures the error that changes from frame to frame. The errors that stay put for seconds, like a centroid biased by a partly hidden board or a camera calibration slightly off, never show up as scatter, and the noise model has to cover them too. The way to tune noise.* for the robot is the same experiment on the robot: hold in front of a prop at a few ranges, with the same script.

Back-to-back detections aren't independent

A Kalman filter assumes every measurement's error is fresh. Camera frames aren't like that. Two frames 100 ms apart see the same water, the same lighting, almost the same viewpoint, and very nearly the same depth-map error, so their errors are mostly shared. Treat ten frames per second as ten independent measurements and the filter becomes overconfident within a second. Its covariance shrinks far below the real error, the gate in Chapter 5 starts rejecting good detections, and covariance checks in autonomy pass on what is really one view.

Mapping handles this per object (inflation): a detection that arrives \Delta t after the last accepted one of the same object has its covariance scaled up by

\Rm \leftarrow \Rm \cdot \min\!\Big(\max\!\big(1,\ \tfrac{\tau}{\Delta t}\big),\ 30\Big), \qquad \tau = 0.3\ \text{s}

Detections more than 0.3 s apart count fully. Closer ones count for \Delta t/\tau of a detection each. The cap of 30 covers several detections of one object in the same frame (\Delta t is floored at 1 ms, so they'd otherwise get 300).

The consequence is worth spelling out. Information is the inverse of variance, and the filter adds it up. Above 1/\tau ≈ 3.3 Hz, each detection brings \Delta t/(\tau\sigma^2) of it, and detections arrive every \Delta t, so the information rate is 1/(\tau\sigma^2) per second whatever the frame rate. Running the detector at 30 Hz instead of 10 doesn't make the estimate converge any faster. Only time spent looking does, or a better viewpoint (smaller \sigma). Below 3.3 Hz every detection counts in full, so a slow detector loses information it never had.

Yaw

Objects also have a heading, estimated by a separate scalar filter per object. The detector reports a surface normal as the detection's +z axis, and mapping takes its heading in the parent frame, \psi = \operatorname{atan2}(n_y, n_x) (mapping.py), with a fixed 3° standard deviation (noise.yaw_std_deg). Unlike position, it doesn't grow with range, even though a plane fit over fewer, noisier points far away is worse. The same correlation inflation applies. Objects with lock_orientation_to_config (the slalom, the bin targets) ignore detected yaw entirely and keep the layout's.

What the model leaves out

  • Bias. A covariance describes scatter, not offset. If the mask's edge consistently bleeds onto the background or the depth map reads 2 % long, every detection is wrong in the same direction, and averaging won't remove it. The covariance keeps shrinking regardless.
  • Correlation between objects. Two objects seen in the same frame share that frame's camera-pose error. Each object's filter treats its own detections as unrelated to the other's.
  • Viewing angle. A board seen edge-on gives a worse plane fit and a worse centroid than one seen face-on. The model only knows range and motion.
  • Wrong-class detections aren't noise at all. A slalom pole labeled as part of the gate is a different object, and no covariance makes it right. Chapter 5's gate and agreement rules and the target lists (Chapter 7) handle those.

None of these is fatal. The gate, the acquisition rules and the course map are all designed to cope with a covariance that is roughly right rather than exact. They do mean a tight covariance should be read as "seen consistently", which isn't always the same as "seen correctly".
