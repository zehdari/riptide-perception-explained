Appendix A

The math in one place

Every equation the detector and mapping run, in the order a detection meets them, with Talos's parameters. Each one has a line in plain words under it, and links back to the chapter that explains it.

How to read the equations

You need vectors, matrices and a little probability. The conventions:

  • Bold letters are vectors or matrices (\bp is a 3D point, \P a 3×3 matrix); plain letters are numbers.
  • A hat marks an estimate: \hat\bx is where mapping thinks something is, \bx where it really is.
  • \mathbf v^\top is the transpose (a column turned into a row), so \mathbf a^\top \mathbf b is the dot product. \P^{-1} is the matrix inverse, I the identity.
  • \leftarrow means “replace with”, as in code: x \leftarrow x + 1.
  • A covariance \P describes an uncertain 3D position: a fuzzy ellipsoid around the estimate. Its diagonal holds the variances (standard deviation squared) along x, y and z.
  • \lVert\mathbf v\rVert^2_{\P^{-1}} = \mathbf v^\top \P^{-1} \mathbf v is a distance measured in standard deviations (the Mahalanobis distance): 1 m counts for little along a direction the ellipsoid is long in, a lot along one it is thin in.
  • \chi^2_3(0.999): the squared Mahalanobis distance a correct 3D measurement stays under 99.9 % of the time. Anything above it is almost certainly a different object or a bad detection.

Notation

Camera optical frame: z forward along the lens axis, x right, y down. The map frame is the course frame: z up, the water surface at z = 0. Odometry positions are in map too; the offset is where the course sits relative to the prior layout.

The camera model

From Chapter 1.

Everything in vision starts by connecting a pixel to a direction in 3D. These are the equations calibration (Chapter 1) measures the numbers for.

A point (x, y, z) in the camera frame lands on pixel (u, v) through the intrinsics:

u = f_x\,\frac{x}{z} + c_x, \qquad v = f_y\,\frac{y}{z} + c_y \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \propto \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{K} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}

In words: a point twice as far away lands twice as close to the image centre. Divide x and y by the distance z, scale by the focal length, shift to the image centre. \propto means “equal up to scale”: K(x, y, z)^\top = (z u,\ z v,\ z), then divide by z. Every point on one ray lands on the same pixel, so a pixel alone gives a direction, not a distance.

With Talos's numbers: Talos's forward camera after rectification has f \approx 1864 px and centre (956, 585). A point 0.5 m right, 0.2 m down and 3 m ahead lands at u = 1864 \cdot 0.5/3 + 956 \approx 1267, v = 1864 \cdot 0.2/3 + 585 \approx 709. Move it to 6 m and it lands at (1111, 647), half as far from the centre.

The lens distortion is applied to the normalized coordinates x' = x/z,\ y' = y/z,\ r^2 = x'^2 + y'^2 first (OpenCV's plumb-bob model, coefficients [k_1, k_2, p_1, p_2, k_3]):

x'' = x'\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + 2p_1 x'y' + p_2(r^2 + 2x'^2), \qquad y'' = y'\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + p_1(r^2 + 2y'^2) + 2p_2 x'y'

In words: real lenses bend straight lines, more the farther from the centre (the r^2, r^4, r^6 terms) and a little sideways if the lens sits tilted (p_1, p_2). Calibration measures the five numbers; the driver undoes the bending so the rest of the code can use the clean pinhole model.

Underwater, rays bend at the flat port by Snell's law, which narrows the view and adds radial distortion, so the camera is calibrated in the water:

n_\text{air} \sin\theta_a = n_\text{water} \sin\theta_w, \qquad n_\text{water} \approx 1.33

In words: light bends where it leaves the water and enters the camera housing, so a lens calibrated in air is wrong underwater.

With Talos's numbers: a ray 30° off the axis in the water is \arcsin(1.33 \cdot 0.5) \approx 41.7° off the axis inside the housing. Near the centre everything looks about 1.33× bigger than in air, and the edges of the view are lost.

From a rectified stereo pair with baseline B, depth and its error from a disparity d:

z = \frac{f\,B}{d}, \qquad \delta z \approx \frac{z^2}{f\,B}\,\delta d

In words: depth comes from how far a point shifts between the left and right image (the disparity d). Far points barely shift, so a one-pixel mistake costs more the farther away the point is: the error grows with distance squared.

With Talos's numbers: the ZED X Mini's baseline is about 50 mm, so at full resolution fB \approx 93 px·m. At 3 m the disparity is 31 px, and a quarter-pixel matching error moves the depth by 9/93 \cdot 0.25 \approx 2.4 cm. At 6 m the disparity is 16 px and the same error costs 9.7 cm.

A mask becomes a pose

From Chapter 4.

YOLO gives a mask of pixels. With the depth image those pixels become 3D points, and a flat prop's points give its position and the way it faces.

Grid-sample the mask every 8 px (at most 500 samples) and back-project each through its depth z:

x = \frac{(u - c_x)\, z}{f_x}, \qquad y = \frac{(v - c_y)\, z}{f_y}, \qquad \bp = (x,\, y,\, z)

In words: the camera model run backwards: a pixel plus its depth gives a 3D point.

Keep the points whose depth is within three robust standard deviations of the median (the depth gate):

m = \operatorname{median}(z_i), \qquad s = \max\!\big(1.4826\,\operatorname{median}|z_i - m|,\; 0.05\ \text{m}\big), \qquad \text{keep } |z_i - m| \le 3\,s

In words: throw away mask samples whose depth is far from the typical depth, e.g. pixels that hit the wall behind the prop. 1.4826 × the median absolute deviation estimates a standard deviation that a few wild values cannot drag around.

With Talos's numbers: the samples' median depth is 2.40 m with a median deviation of 1 cm. 1.4826 \cdot 0.01 = 1.5 cm is below the 5 cm floor, so s = 5 cm and every sample between 2.25 and 2.55 m is kept: the pool wall at 4 m behind the prop goes, a slightly tilted edge at 2.43 m stays. The floor matters for clean surfaces: with a near-zero spread, the gate would otherwise reject points a few millimetres off.

then a radius pass (more than \min(10, 0.8n) neighbours within 1 m) and a statistical pass (mean distance to the k nearest neighbours below the mean plus one standard deviation). The plane is the direction of least spread of what is left:

\bar\bp = \frac1n \sum_i \bp_i, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} (\bp_1 - \bar\bp)^\top \\ \vdots \\ (\bp_n - \bar\bp)^\top \end{bmatrix} = U \Sigma V^\top, \qquad \bn = V_{:,3}, \quad \bn \leftarrow -\bn \text{ if } n_z > 0

In words: fit a flat surface through the points. The SVD finds the three directions the points spread along; a flat prop spreads in two, and the third, the one with the least spread, is its surface normal \bn. It is flipped, if needed, to face the camera.

With Talos's numbers: a board facing the camera spreads about 30 cm in x and y but only a centimetre in z. The third direction is then close to (0, 0, \pm 1), and the sign is chosen so it is (0, 0, -1), pointing back at the camera.

The published position is the box centre (u_b, v_b) back-projected at the points' mean depth, and the orientation puts body +z on the normal:

\mathbf c = \Big(\tfrac{(u_b - c_x)\, \bar z}{f_x},\; \tfrac{(v_b - c_y)\, \bar z}{f_y},\; \bar z\Big), \qquad R = \big[\, \mathbf e_1 \;\; \mathbf e_2 \;\; \bn \,\big], \quad \mathbf e_1 = \frac{\bn \times \mathbf a}{\lVert \bn \times \mathbf a \rVert},\ \mathbf e_2 = \bn \times \mathbf e_1

In words: the position is the middle of the detection box pushed out to the surface's depth. The orientation is built from the normal plus a second axis that is never parallel to it, so the frame is always well defined.

\mathbf a is the camera x axis, or y when the normal is within 26° of x; the first column is negated if \det R < 0.

The torpedo board

From Chapter 5.

Small symbols and holes have few pixels and poor depth on their own. Fitting one plane to the whole board and placing every symbol on it gives each a much better position.

One plane is fitted to the pooled points of every symbol on the board (fire and blood required, ambulance and fire engine optional), anchored at the mean symbol centroid \mathbf a. Each symbol and circle centre is then intersected with that plane along its camera ray:

\mathbf d = \frac{K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^\top}{\lVert K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^\top \rVert}, \qquad t = \frac{\bn \cdot \mathbf a}{\bn \cdot \mathbf d}, \qquad \mathbf p = t\,\mathbf d

In words: turn each pixel into a direction \mathbf d from the camera, then slide along it until it hits the board's plane (at distance t). This places small things, like the holes, on the board even when their own depth is poor.

With an in-plane basis (\mathbf e_u, \mathbf e_v), the board centre is the middle of the projected points' extent, and the four holes split two and two along the fire-to-blood axis:

\mathbf c = \mathbf p_0 + \tfrac12\big(\min_j u_j + \max_j u_j\big)\,\mathbf e_u + \tfrac12\big(\min_j v_j + \max_j v_j\big)\,\mathbf e_v, \qquad s_h = (\mathbf p_h - \mathbf p_\text{fire}) \cdot \frac{\mathbf p_\text{blood} - \mathbf p_\text{fire}}{\lVert \mathbf p_\text{blood} - \mathbf p_\text{fire} \rVert}

In words: the board centre is halfway between its outermost symbols. s_h measures how far each hole is along the line from fire to blood, so the two holes nearest fire belong to fire.

The two smallest s_h are fire's holes; of each pair, the one nearer its symbol is the large hole.

How much a detection is worth

From Chapter 6.

Mapping weights each detection by how much to trust it. That trust is a covariance, built from range, motion and timing.

Standard deviation across the camera ray and along it, at range r, with the vehicle turning at \omega and moving at v and a stamp error t_s:

\sigma_\perp^2 = (b + k_\perp r)^2 + (\omega\, t_s\, r)^2, \qquad \sigma_\parallel = b + k_\parallel\, r + k_2\, r^2

In words: a detection is less certain the farther away it is, more so along the camera ray (depth) than across it, and more while the robot turns or moves, because the image and the robot's pose are never stamped at exactly the same moment.

With Talos's numbers: at 3 m, holding still: \sigma_\perp = 0.01 + 0.003 \cdot 3 = 1.9 cm across the ray, \sigma_\parallel = 0.01 + 0.008 \cdot 3 + 0.003 \cdot 9 = 6.1 cm along it. Turning at 0.3 rad/s adds 0.3 \cdot 0.03 \cdot 3 = 2.7 cm across, for \sigma_\perp = \sqrt{1.9^2 + 2.7^2} \approx 3.3 cm.

\Rm_\text{cam} = \sigma_\perp^2 I + (\sigma_\parallel^2 - \sigma_\perp^2)\,\hat u \hat u^\top + (v t_s)^2 I, \qquad \Rm = \Rm_{pc}\, \Rm_\text{cam}\, \Rm_{pc}^\top

In words: this turns the two standard deviations into a cigar-shaped ellipsoid pointing along the ray (\hat u\hat u^\top picks the along-ray direction), then rotates it from the camera into the object's frame.

where \Rm_{pc} rotates the camera frame into the object's parent frame. A detection \Delta t after the object's last accepted one shares most of its error, so

\Rm \leftarrow \Rm \cdot \min\!\Big(\max\!\big(1,\ \tfrac{\tau}{\Delta t}\big),\ 30\Big), \qquad \Delta t \ge 1\ \text{ms}

In words: thirty detections of the same object a fraction of a second apart are not thirty independent measurements, so each counts for less: covariance is inflated up to 30× for detections closer together than \tau.

With Talos's numbers: detections 0.1 s apart have their covariance tripled (0.3 / 0.1); 1 s apart, not at all. Ten detections in one second count about as much as three independent ones.

Detected yaw is the heading of the normal in the parent frame, \operatorname{atan2}(n_y, n_x), with a fixed 3° standard deviation, inflated the same way.

Tracking one object

From Chapter 7.

Each object keeps a running estimate. New detections update it if they fit, are rejected if they don't, and a new track only starts when several detections agree.

A three-state Kalman update in the object's parent frame, in Joseph form:

\boldsymbol\nu = \bz - \hat\bx, \quad \mathbf S = \P + \Rm, \quad \K = \P\mathbf S^{-1}, \qquad \hat\bx \leftarrow \hat\bx + \K\boldsymbol\nu, \qquad \P \leftarrow (I - \K)\,\P\,(I - \K)^\top + \K\Rm\K^\top

In words: \boldsymbol\nu is the surprise: how far the detection is from the estimate. The gain \K decides how much of it to believe: when the track is uncertain (\P large) and the detection good (\Rm small) the estimate jumps most of the way, in the opposite case it barely moves. \P then shrinks. The “Joseph form” is the same update written so rounding errors cannot make the covariance invalid.

With Talos's numbers: one axis, track at \sigma = 25 cm (P = 0.0625 m²), detection at 4 cm (R = 0.0016). K = 0.0625 / 0.0641 = 0.975: the estimate moves 97.5 % of the way to the detection, and P \leftarrow (1 - K)P \approx 0.0016, so \sigma \approx 4 cm. The next equally good detection gets K \approx 0.49, half the way. After n such detections \sigma \approx 4/\sqrt n cm.

Once the track has 10 accepted detections, a detection is rejected when

\boldsymbol\nu^\top \mathbf S^{-1} \boldsymbol\nu \;>\; 16.27 \;=\; \chi^2_3(0.999)

In words: reject a detection that is too far from the estimate, measured in standard deviations of both together (\mathbf S = \P + \Rm).

With Talos's numbers: \sqrt{16.27} \approx 4: in 3D, about four standard deviations. If \mathbf S amounts to 5 cm on every axis, a detection more than about 20 cm from the estimate is rejected.

and 30 rejections in a row restart the track. Yaw is a scalar filter on the wrapped innovation \operatorname{wrap}(\psi_z - \hat\psi), gated at \chi^2_1(0.999) = 10.83. A map object's track ages between sightings by the odometry drift since it was last seen, \P \leftarrow \P + \Q(\Delta s, \Delta t) (below).

Acquisition. Until a track has accepted a detection, detections are held for 4 s. Two agree when

\lVert \bz_a - \bz_b \rVert \;\le\; \max\!\Big(0.3\ \text{m},\ 3\sqrt{\lambda_\text{max}(\Rm_a + \Rm_b)}\Big)

In words: two detections agree if they are within 30 cm, or within three standard deviations when they are noisier than that.

With Talos's numbers: two detections at 3 m (\sigma_\parallel = 6.1 cm each): 3\sqrt{2 \cdot 0.061^2} = 26 cm, under the 30 cm floor, so they agree within 30 cm. At 5 m, \sigma_\parallel = 12.5 cm and the radius grows to 53 cm.

The largest group of detections that agree with one member starts the track once it has 4 members. It needs 12, spanning at least 2 s, when its mean \bar\bz is far from where the map expects the object:

(\bar\bz - \hat\bp)^\top \Big(\boldsymbol\Sigma_{\hat\bp} + \tfrac1m \textstyle\sum_{j} \Rm_j\Big)^{-1} (\bar\bz - \hat\bp) \;>\; 16.27

In words: if the agreeing group is far from where the map expected the object (beyond the 99.9 % gate), it might be a false detection, so it needs more evidence: 12 detections over 2 s instead of 4.

The course map

From Chapter 8.

Single tracks drift with odometry and can be fooled. The map solves for all props together: one shift for the whole course, plus a small correction per prop.

Every map-parented object is the prior layout, turned by the course yaw, plus where the course sits in odometry, plus that object's own layout error:

\bp_i = R_\psi\,\bp_i^{\,0} + \bo + \bd_i

In words: where prop i really is = the measured layout, turned by any heading error of the whole course, shifted by where the course actually sits, plus a small correction for that prop alone.

The offset random-walks with distance travelled and time:

\Q(\Delta s, \Delta t) = \operatorname{diag}\big(q_{s}\Delta s + q_{t}\Delta t,\ \ q_{s}\Delta s + q_{t}\Delta t,\ \ q_{s,z}\Delta s + q_{t,z}\Delta t\big)

In words: odometry slowly drifts, so the course's apparent position can wander: its variance grows a little with every metre driven (q_s) and every second (q_t). \operatorname{diag} is a matrix with those numbers on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.

With Talos's numbers: 20 m of driving adds 0.002 \cdot 20 = 0.04 m² of variance in x and y (a 20 cm standard deviation); 100 s of hovering adds only 0.001 m² (3 cm).

A track with 10 detections becomes a fix (\bz_k, \P_k) at the path length s_k it was last seen. Two fixes of one object less than 1 m of travel apart merge:

\P = \left(\P_a^{-1} + \P_b^{-1}\right)^{-1}, \qquad \bz = \P\left(\P_a^{-1}\bz_a + \P_b^{-1}\bz_b\right)

In words: two looks at the same object combine like a weighted average, each weighted by how certain it is (the inverse covariance). The result is more certain than either.

With Talos's numbers: fixes with 5 cm and 10 cm standard deviations: (1/25 + 1/100)^{-1} = 20 cm², so 4.5 cm, and the combined position is 80 % the better fix and 20 % the other.

With the fixes sorted by path length, one offset node per fix, one deviation per object seen, and the course yaw, the solve minimizes

J = \frac{\psi^2}{\sigma_\psi^2} + \sum_k \big\|\bz_k - R_\psi\bp^{\,0}_{i(k)} - \bo_k - \bd_{i(k)}\big\|^2_{\P_k^{-1}} + \|\bo_1\|^2_{(\boldsymbol\Sigma_o + \Q_1)^{-1}} + \sum_{k} \|\bo_{k+1} - \bo_k\|^2_{\Q_{k}^{-1}} + \sum_i \rho\!\left(\|\bd_i\|^2_{\boldsymbol\Sigma_L^{-1}}\right)

In words: J is a total penalty that the solve makes as small as possible. Term by term: the course heading should be close to the layout's; every fix should match the model above; the course offset should start near zero and change only as much as drift allows between fixes; and each prop should sit near its layout position. Every term is a squared distance in standard deviations, so a well-measured sighting outweighs a vague one.

With Talos's numbers: a fix 10 cm from where the model puts it, with a 5 cm standard deviation, adds (10/5)^2 = 4 to J. A prop 25 cm from its layout position (one \sigma_L) adds about 1.

\Q_k is the drift between consecutive fixes (plus 10^{-6} on the diagonal), \Q_1 the drift up to the first, \boldsymbol\Sigma_o the offset's start prior and \boldsymbol\Sigma_L the layout prior.

The layout term is Cauchy-robust, solved by iteratively reweighted least squares (the rotation linearized about the current \psi each pass):

\rho(r^2) = s^2 \log\!\left(1 + \frac{r^2}{s^2}\right), \qquad w(r^2) = \frac{1}{1 + r^2/s^2}, \qquad s = 2

In words: a plain squared penalty would let one badly placed prop drag the whole map. \rho grows only slowly for large errors, so a prop far from its layout gets a low weight w and stops pulling on the others. “Iteratively reweighted” means: solve, recompute the weights, solve again.

With Talos's numbers: with r in layout standard deviations (25 cm), a prop 25 cm off keeps w = 1/(1 + 1/4) = 0.8; 50 cm off gives w = 0.5, the release threshold; 1 m off gives w = 0.2. So a prop more than about 50 cm from where the layout says is released and placed by its own sightings.

A new set of fixes is solved from zero, from every start that blames one prior (its weight set to 0 for the first pass), and from the last answer; the lowest J wins. An object with w < 0.5 is released. Every object's estimate, seen or not, is then

\hat\bp_i = R_{\hat\psi}\,\bp_i^{\,0} + \hat\bo + \hat\bd_i, \qquad \boldsymbol\Sigma_i = \mathbf J\,\mathbf A^{-1}_{[\psi,\bo,\bd_i]}\,\mathbf J^\top + \Q(\Delta s, \Delta t)\;\big[\,+\,\boldsymbol\Sigma_L \text{ if unseen}\big]

In words: the published estimate of each prop is the model above with the solved values. Its uncertainty comes from the solve's uncertainty in heading, offset and that prop's correction, plus the drift since the last sighting, plus the full layout uncertainty for a prop not seen yet.

\mathbf A is the normal matrix at the solution, \mathbf J = [\,\partial (R_\psi\bp^{\,0}_i)/\partial\psi \;\; I \;\; I\,], and \Delta s, \Delta t the travel since the newest fix.

What is published

From Chapter 9.

What autonomy sees: each object's estimate and covariance. The covariance is how the tree knows when an object is trustworthy.

Until an object is confirmed by fresh detections while targeted (10, or 3 that agree with a known estimate), its published covariance is floored:

\boldsymbol\Sigma_\text{pub} = \boldsymbol\Sigma + \operatorname{diag}\big(\max(1 - \Sigma_{jj},\ 0)\big), \qquad \sigma^2_{\text{roll}} = \sigma^2_{\text{pitch}} = 0.1, \qquad \sigma^2_\text{yaw} \ge 0.1

In words: until autonomy has looked at an object on purpose, its published uncertainty is kept at 1 m or more, so a tree waiting for a tight covariance never acts on an estimate nobody checked.

With Talos's numbers: an estimate good to 4 cm (\Sigma_{jj} = 0.0016 m²) but not yet confirmed is published with 0.0016 + 0.9984 = 1 m², a 1 m standard deviation. A tree waiting for the covariance to drop keeps waiting until the object is targeted and confirmed.
