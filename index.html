How Talos sees

Perception and mapping for an AUV

Talos's whole software stack, cameras to behaviour tree, running the team's RoboSub 2026 mission in the Nereus simulator, eight times faster than real time. Every prop it swims to, it found with the code this site explains.

riptide_perception, chapter by chapter

Every prop Talos swims to in that run, it located with the two halves of riptide_perception: a detector that finds props in each camera image, and a map that collects those detections into where each prop really is.

Talos is the Underwater Robotics Team's competition AUV. Every RoboSub task, from swimming through the gate to dropping markers in the bins, starts with the same question: where exactly is the thing? riptide_perception answers it. This site is written for a new team member: it walks through how that code works, why it is built the way it is, and how to work on it yourself. Nothing is assumed beyond some Python and high-school vectors; the maths is introduced where it is used, and it is all collected on one page.

The package has two halves, and so does the site.

  • Vision (tensor_detector) looks at one camera image at a time and says what it sees and where that is relative to the camera. To do that the cameras have to be calibrated, and a neural network has to be trained to recognise the props.
  • Mapping (riptide_mapping) collects those detections over the whole run and keeps a map of the course: where every prop is, how sure it is, and an explanation for why the course isn't where the map said. It publishes each prop as a coordinate frame that the behaviour trees and the controller steer by.

The run above

The video is one run of RepairCompMpcTree, the tree the team runs at competition, with the course placed exactly where Talos's map says. Talos passes the gate on its prior map, maps the slalom and the torpedo board, fires at the board's holes, finds both table baskets, centres the table on them, picks up each of the four objects and carries it to its basket, drops both markers in the bins, works the magnets, and swims home through the gate. Each of those steps waits on a mapping estimate getting precise enough (Chapter 9 lists every threshold). Chapter 10 replays the same run from above with every detection and estimate, and then repeats it on a course moved off the map, where two things go wrong.

The chapters

Vision

  1. Cameras and calibration. How a camera turns the world into pixels, why the factory calibration is wrong underwater, and how the team calibrates with a ChArUco board.
  2. Training the detector. Recording the props, labelling masks with SAM, Nereus's synthetic images, and the augmentations that keep the model honest.
  3. The detector, frame by frame. Pairing each image with its depth map, running YOLO, and what gets published.
  4. From a mask to a pose. Sampling the mask, back-projecting through the depth, throwing out the background, and fitting a plane.
  5. Props made of several pieces. The torpedo board and its holes, the gate signs and the slalom poles.

Mapping

  1. How far to trust a detection. A covariance for every detection, from range, bearing and how fast the robot is moving.
  2. Tracking one object. A Kalman filter per prop, the gate that rejects lookalikes, and why the first detections wait until they agree.
  3. The course map. One shared offset for odometry drift, a deviation per prop for layout error, and a robust solve that decides which is which.
  4. Frames, targets and the mission tree. What mapping publishes and what RepairCompMpcTree asks of it, task by task.
  5. One mission in Nereus. The run above from overhead, every estimate against the truth, and a second run on a moved course where two things go wrong.
