Talos is the Underwater Robotics Team's competition AUV. To score at RoboSub it has to swim through a gate, around slalom poles, fire torpedoes through holes in a board, drop markers in bins and pick objects off a table, and every one of those tasks starts with the same question: where exactly is the thing? riptide_perception answers it. This site explains how, from the pixels to the coordinate frames the autonomy and the controller steer by.

The package has two halves. tensor_detector looks at camera images and says what it sees and where it is relative to the camera, one frame at a time. riptide_mapping collects those detections over the whole run and keeps a map of the course: an estimate of every prop's position, how sure it is, and an explanation for why the course isn't where the map said it would be. Already know Kalman filters and least squares? All the math is on one page, with Talos's parameters.

Why it is harder than it looks

A neural network that outlines the torpedo board in an image is the well-trodden part: train a YOLO model on labelled pictures and it will. What makes underwater perception hard is everything after the outline:

  • The box is 2D. The board is 3D, tilted, and partly hidden. The detector has to pick the right depth pixels, reject the ones that belong to the pool wall behind the board, and fit a surface. (Chapters 2 and 3.)
  • One detection is noisy, and how noisy depends on range. Stereo depth error grows with the square of distance, and a timestamp a few milliseconds off smears a detection sideways while the robot turns. (Chapter 4.)
  • The detector is sometimes wrong. Slalom poles look like gate legs, the two bin vinyls are the same class, a reflection is a pole for one frame. Mapping has to ignore a stray detection without ignoring a prop that really is somewhere unexpected. (Chapter 5.)
  • The map is wrong too. The prior layout comes from measuring the course by hand at the competition, and the robot's own odometry drifts metres over a run. A sighting of one prop has to correct the others, but a single badly measured prop must not drag the whole map with it. (Chapter 6.)

The chapters take these in order. Every interactive figure runs a JavaScript port of the team's code: the detector's geometry and the mapping estimator, line for line, checked against the Python by a test suite. The real data comes from the Nereus simulator running Talos's full software stack: its cameras, the YOLO models, mapping, the behaviour trees and the MPC, exactly as on the robot.

The chapters

  1. Cameras and the detector. Two stereo cameras, two YOLO segmentation models, and how each image is paired with the depth map taken at the same instant.
  2. From a mask to a pose. Sampling the mask, back-projecting through the depth, throwing out the background, and fitting a plane.
  3. Props made of several pieces. The torpedo board, the gate halves, the table and the slalom poles: detections that only make sense together.
  4. How far to trust a detection. A covariance for every detection, from range, bearing and how fast the robot is moving.
  5. Tracking one object. A Kalman filter per prop, the gate that rejects lookalikes, and why the first detections wait until they agree.
  6. The course map. One shared offset for odometry drift, a deviation per prop for layout error, and a robust solve that decides which is which.
  7. Frames, targets and autonomy. What mapping publishes, how the behaviour trees drive it, and the special cases: two identical bins and a table fit to its baskets.
  8. One mission in Nereus. The replay above, slowed down: every estimate against the truth, and what mapping said at each step.
